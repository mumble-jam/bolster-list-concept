Content

How Bolster's screens are worded: sentence case, the screen's own words, page titles that match the menu, and values that read as what they are, with ids exactly as the source writes them.

Needs workHigh priorityrecipes/list/rules.jsoncomponents/states.csscomponents/toast.cssUpdated 2026-11-02

Words are part of the interface. The rules here keep them plain and predictable: the same thing has the same name everywhere, a label says what will happen, and a value reads the way a contractor would say it. Many of them are enforced by the list recipe's rules (L-case, L-words, L-types, L-source), so built pages can't drift.

Sentence case

Everything is in sentence case: page titles, headings, buttons, menu items, tabs, column headers, statuses, filter chips. Only the first word and proper names take a capital. Names of people, companies and products keep theirs: Lakeside Drywall, QuickBooks, Bolster.

The screen's own words

Copy that explains what's on screen (an info popover, a helper line, an empty state, a tooltip) names things exactly as the screen labels them, in the same case (D27). If a card says Booked, the explanation says Booked, never “won”; if a status is Declined, never “lost”.

  • Define a term once when the screen doesn't show it, in the screen's labels: “Won is Booked plus In progress”. Then use it the same way everywhere, the card's own figures included (“62 of 85 decided”).
  • On-screen terms stand out in an explanation: a status carries its mark, as on its card; any other label is set in medium weight.
  • Borrowed terms keep their list's labels. A customer's lifetime value adds up their jobs, so it says what the job list says.

Page titles

A page's title is the label of the menu item that opens it, word for word (D28): Job list, not Jobs; Leads and customers, not Customers. The section's name (Jobs) is already the heading of the menu right above.

  • The browser tab follows: “Job list · Bolster”.
  • Pages read their title from the menu, never type their own, so renaming a menu item renames its page.
  • Copy that needs a plural uses the list's noun, not its title: “Jobs you create show up here”, “132 jobs”, “Delete 3 jobs?”.
  • A page that isn't a menu item, such as a job or an estimate, takes the record's name: “Garage drywall”.

Buttons and actions

A button's label is a verb and what it acts on: “Create invoice”, “Schedule visit”, “Preview and send”. It says what will happen, so nobody has to guess what “OK” or “Submit” does.

  • Create or add. “Create <noun>” for records the business makes (a job, an invoice, a vendor order); “Add <noun>” for records it keeps (a customer, a catalog item).
  • While it runs, the label says what it's doing: “Saving…”, “Sending…”.
  • A confirmation names the outcome on both sides: “Archive job” and “Keep job”, not “Yes” and “No”. Focus starts on the safe one.
  • A tooltip is a short phrase with no full stop, the same words as the control's accessible name: “Refresh”, “More actions” (I4).

How values read

A value reads as its type, wherever it shows (D59). In a row's name lines or a record's heading, it's written as its column would show it: “5 items”, “$1,900.00”, “Labor and materials”, never a bare number.

  • Identifiers are written exactly as the source writes them. People recognise and search them, so a vendor order is “#0003” and an invoice “Invoice #29599975514”, as Bolster shows them. A format is never invented: no “EST-2136” where the product has no such number.
  • Each value says whose it is (D60). A vendor order's “#0003” is the vendor order's own number, never the job's, so the record, the menus and the search hint call it the vendor order number. The job list shows no job number, since Bolster's has none.
  • A label that joins several things' values is read apart (D61). “Vendor 100001846 #0003” is the vendor's name and the vendor order's number; “$1,900 • 5 items • new home” is the order's total, its item count and the job's name. Each part is placed and worded on its own.
  • One place per row (D61). A value with its own column isn't repeated on the name's second line. On a phone, where columns go, the row's bottom line shows the first of them, so nothing is lost.

Empty states and errors

Say what happened, then what someone can do about it, in the list's noun. These come from the components, and are the pattern to follow until a fuller guide is written.

  • Empty: a heading that says what's missing (“No jobs yet”), one line on how things get here, and the action that adds one.
  • No results: name what's narrowing the list, in the screen's words, and offer the way back (“Clear all filters”).
  • Failed: what didn't happen, and the next step: “Jobs didn't load. Check your connection and try again.”
  • A field's error says what to enter, with an example, not only that it's wrong.
  • A toast says what was done, to whom: “Proposal sent to Mykola Khvylovy”, with Undo where it can be undone.

Name a thing for what it does

A part does one job, and its words say which. Global search finds things; it doesn't create them (D123). Its panel holds the types to search and what was opened recently, never “Create job” or “Add customer” tiles. Making something stays where it's made, on each page's own Create button. The same goes for a search field's placeholder: it lists what it searches, “Search by client, job, address or number”, never a bare “Search”.

What's still open

  • No voice and tone guide: how Bolster sounds to a contractor, a client and a sub.
  • Formats are decided for what lists show (D59), not written down as one table: money, dates, times, ranges, addresses, phone numbers, quantities and units.
  • No guidance for error messages, confirmations and empty states' words beyond the examples in the components.
